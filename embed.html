In December 2024, the General Assembly adopted resolution 79/192 on the working methods of the Third Committee, with the objective of ‘achiev[ing] a manageable workload for the Committee and ensur[ing] the high quality of its deliberations’. The document, ‘Implementation of General Assembly Resolution 79/192 – Elements for a Roadmap’, requested in operative paragraph 3 of the resolution, was presented and discussed, but the General Assembly never formally adopted it.

Nevertheless, the Roadmap discussions established a set of guiding principles aimed at progressively reducing the number of mandates reporting to each session of the General Assembly’s Third Committee by an indicative 3 to 5 mandates per year, until the Committee’s 84th session. This would amount to a total reduction of approximately 12 to 20 reports over the 81st to 84th sessions of the General Assembly, or 9 to 15 reports over the three sessions covered by this analysis (GA81 to GA83).

At the same time, successive efficiency initiatives, the UN’s ongoing budgetary constraints, and the UN80 reform process have further prompted Member States to advance proposals to reduce the reporting periodicity of Special Procedures to the General Assembly.

As highlighted by civil society organisations, without a strategic approach that includes meaningful consultation with relevant actors and a case-by-case assessment that highlights the extraordinary nature of these measures, these developments risk diminishing the visibility of key human rights issues in New York, widening the institutional gap between the General Assembly and the Human Rights Council, and normalising this practice over the long term. Reductions in reporting are accompanied by corresponding reductions in resources allocated from the Regular Budget to Special Procedures, which are exacerbated by reductions in extra-budgetary contributions to Special Procedures mandates.

The analysis below provides an overview of reductions in reporting implemented so far within the time-frame proposed by the Roadmap: General Assembly sessions 81 to 83 (2026–2028), and it considers the reductions adopted by the Council from its 60th session (HRC60) in September 2025 to its 63rd session (HRC63) in September–October 2026.

Main findings

  1. The scale of reductions has already exceeded what was contemplated under the Roadmap. Resolutions adopted at HRC60–63 mean 19 fewer reports to GA81–83. That is above the 9 to 15 reports the Roadmap’s indicative pace implies for those three sessions, and only one short of the 20-report ceiling for its whole period to GA84. The General Assembly’s 82nd session (GA82, 2027) is most affected, with 9 fewer reports; GA81 (2026) and GA83 (2028) each have 5 fewer.
  2. What began as a workload-management exercise risks becoming a structural change to Special Procedures reporting. Several resolutions do not merely omit reporting for a specified session but establish biennial reporting, potentially halving GA reporting over successive mandate renewals. There’s no uniform language around reporting reductions. Some resolutions state the specific sessions where mandate holders are meant to report to the GA, and others just vaguely state they should be reporting biennially to the General Assembly, only to be defined by the programme budget implications (PBI) arising from the resolution.
  3. Safeguards remain inconsistent. Some resolutions preserve alternative opportunities for GA engagement or redirect savings to substantive mandate activities, while others contain no equivalent protection.
  4. Between the 60th and 63rd sessions of the Human Rights Council (HRC60–HRC63), 13 of the 33 Special Procedures mandates up for renewal (39%) saw their reporting to the General Assembly reduced, merged or ended. Eleven had their reporting periodicity reduced, one was merged and one was terminated. The share was highest at HRC61 (5 of 10) and HRC62 (4 of 8), compared with 2 of 8 at HRC60 and 2 of 7 at HRC63.
  5. States in the Western European and Others Group (WEOG) have led this reporting reduction process thus far. WEOG States led 10 of the 13 changes, either alone (5) or within cross-regional core groups that include at least one WEOG member (5).
  6. Close to three in ten thematic Special Procedures mandates that submit written reports to the General Assembly (12 of 41) have already been affected by the current initiatives to reduce reporting.

In depth analysis

Overview of Special Procedures reporting to the General Assembly and the Human Rights Council since 2014

There were 39 thematic mandates in 2014 and 46 thematic mandates in 2025. As of October 2026, there are 44 mandates (following the merger of the mandates on trafficking and slavery, decided in 2025, and the termination of the mandate on leprosy in 2026), representing an increase of around 13% over 12 years.

Since the start of 2014, nine new thematic mandates were established; two of them were created during 2014 and are already included in that year’s count of 39. During the same period, one mandate was terminated and two thematic mandates were merged into one.

How has thematic reporting to the General Assembly and Human Rights Council changed since 2014?

There has been a 36% increase in Special Procedures reporting to the General Assembly, rising from 36 reports in 2014 to 49 reports in 2025. These totals cover all Special Procedures, including the country mandates that report to the Assembly. Most of the growth came before 2018, when the total reached 46; since then it has been broadly stable. The increase reflects the growth in the number of mandates, as well as the fact that individual mandates may be required to present more than one report to the General Assembly in a given year.

It is also important to note that:

  • The Working Group on Arbitrary Detention is not required to report to the General Assembly.
  • The Working Group on Enforced or Involuntary Disappearances and the Working Group on Discrimination against Women and Girls only present orally to the Assembly, without written reports.
  • Country visit reports are not presented to the General Assembly.

The largest difference, however, comes from country visit reports, which make up around half of the reports presented to the Human Rights Council each year and are not presented to the General Assembly.

How many General Assembly reports have been cut?

The reductions adopted during HRC60, HRC61, HRC62 and HRC63 have resulted in fewer reports to the General Assembly across three sessions.

19 reports have been cut in total across GA81, GA82 and GA83: five in 2026, nine in 2027 and five in 2028. Six of the 19 result from the merger of the slavery and trafficking mandates and the termination of the leprosy mandate (three each).

The Third Committee’s Roadmap envisaged progressive reductions through to GA84. Resolutions adopted during the past four Human Rights Council sessions (HRC60–63) have already generated a cumulative reduction of 19 reports by GA83. That is more than the 9 to 15 the Roadmap’s pace implies for those three sessions, and only one short of the 20-report ceiling for the whole period, with GA84 still to come.

What is the impact on thematic balance and visibility?

One of the primary concerns raised by civil society organisations is the thematic balance of reporting reductions and how these may negatively affect the visibility of certain issues at the General Assembly.

Reporting reductions were broadly distributed across the three thematic clusters. In each cluster, roughly four in ten of the mandates up for renewal were affected: 5 of 13 in development, economic, social and cultural rights (DESC), 3 of 7 in equality and non-discrimination (END), and 5 of 13 in rule of law, accountability and civil space (RACS). Each cluster’s share of the cuts closely mirrors its share of renewals and of all thematic mandates, so no cluster has so far been disproportionately affected.

Who are the main actors driving changes to reporting practices?

WEOG States, along with cross-regional groups that include at least one WEOG country, are the main penholders driving changes to reporting practices. WEOG States led 5 of the 13 changes on their own, and 5 of the 6 cross-regional initiatives included at least one WEOG member: 10 of 13 in total.

There are notable exceptions:

  • The termination of the mandate of the Special Rapporteur on the elimination of discrimination against persons affected by leprosy was championed by a core group comprising Japan, Brazil, Ecuador, Ethiopia, Fiji, India, Kyrgyzstan and Morocco.
  • Two initiatives to reduce reporting were led by the African Group during HRC63.

What language is used in resolutions to reduce reporting periodicity?

There is no uniform language around reporting reductions. Some resolutions specify the sessions in which mandate holders should report; others state more generally that they should report biennially to the General Assembly. Some resolutions use biennial reporting language while also specifying the sessions concerned.

Risks of indefinite biennial reporting

Language that biennialises reporting, rather than cutting a specific session, presents two risks:

  1. Greater cumulative reductions: over a six-year period (two renewals), it would reduce reporting by half, compared with a one-third reduction if a single session were cut per three-year renewal period.
  2. Less flexibility: it would remove the possibility of choosing which years are most appropriate for reducing reporting, such as following the appointment of a new mandate holder or when a mandate holder considers it strategically appropriate.

What does ISHR recommend for future changes to reporting requirements?

The analysis demonstrates the need for a more strategic and coordinated approach to any further consideration of changes to Special Procedures reporting.

Reporting reductions should not be treated as an automatic or purely numerical means of reducing the Third Committee’s workload. Instead, they should be assessed individually and cumulatively in light of their impact on mandate implementation, the visibility of human rights issues at the General Assembly, and opportunities for engagement with mandate holders.
